# Cover note — send with the adviser pack

Subject: **20 minutes? Looking for where we've misread a university's rules**

---

Hi [name],

I've built a small tool for NSW students — you enter your HSC subjects and it tells you which
STEM degrees you're prepared for and what any gap actually costs. It's for my own son in Year 11
first, but it's public and free and no one pays to be in it.

Every rule in it is copied word for word from a university's own page, and software checks the
quotes are real. That part is handled.

**What I can't check is whether we've understood any of them correctly.** We've already got two
wrong. Both times the quote was perfect — we'd just read Sydney's *recommended studies* column as
*assumed knowledge*, and read straight past a Macquarie page that says "Assumed knowledge: None"
and invented a requirement the university explicitly disclaims.

So the ask is narrow:

> **Please mark any sentence you wouldn't say to a family in a Year 11 confirmation meeting, and
> tell me what you'd say instead.**

Nineteen items, about twenty minutes. There's a "true but misleading" option as well as
"wrong" — that's the one I most expect to need. One disagreement is worth more to me than
eighteen ticks.

Nothing is sent anywhere; the page has no server, your answers stay in your browser, and there's
a button that builds a plain-text summary you can paste back into an email. It prints, if you'd
rather mark it up on paper.

## One bias to name if you want to

A selective-school adviser systematically over-weights scaling, Extension 2 and early entry, and
under-weights *"you can still get the offer without Physics."* That is useful signal, not noise —
but it is not the whole population the tool serves. If a second reviewer is cheap, make them a
comprehensive-school adviser with a wide ATAR range. **The first disagreement from each population
is a different disagreement.**

## Who to ask

A careers adviser who **still runs the meetings**, not only manages the faculty. Faculty heads
remember policy; meeting-runners remember what parents actually hear.
